Eight trays, two shelves, left to right. Nothing else. This is what goes on the language shelf after CVC decoding, in the order a child meets it, with the exact contents of every tray.

8 trays · 12 pictures · about ten minutes

The one rule

Every tray moves a sound out of the child’s mouth, onto the table, then onto paper.

That is the whole shelf. If a tray does not do one of those three moves, it does not belong here — put it on the reading shelf instead.

Reading collapses letters into a word. Encoding runs the other way: the child starts with a sound stuck in his own head and has to get it out. It leaves his mouth, becomes something he can push around with his hands, and only then becomes a pencil mark. Skip the middle move and you get a child who reads fluently and freezes at a blank page.

01 · The shelf map

Two shelves. Four trays each. That is the whole language area.

Left to right is hardest-last. A child works across the top shelf, then across the one below, and never chooses from the far right until he has been shown it.

How to read this page

Every tray below has four tabs. Quick is the card — what is on the tray and what the child does; that is all you need to pack the shelf. Go deeper is the thinking behind it — why the tray is shaped the way it is, and what to do when it goes wrong; Explain More is exactly what to make and exactly what to do, spelled out step by step. Build it is the audited shopping and printing list for that one tray — exact quantities, cut sizes, and the order to assemble it in. The trays are in order, and a child works on at most two next-door trays at a time — never on four.

Eight is a ceiling, not a target. If you can only get six made, run six — a shelf with two half-built trays teaches the children that materials are optional.

Two decisions I made for you

There is no separate phonogram tray. Heart words and the first phonograms live on a small ring inside Tray 4, because dictation is where a child discovers that some words cannot be sounded out. And grammar comes last, on the smallest tray, played out loud before it is ever a written exercise.

Why this tray

Joseph (2000). At-risk kindergartners taught with word boxes gained more in phoneme awareness, letter-sound knowledge and spelling than children taught the same content without them.

What problem this tray solves

Mia can read the word pig off a card. But say “pig” and ask her to write it and she writes pg. She is not being careless. She catches the /p/ at the front and the /g/ at the end, and the vowel in the middle goes past too fast to hear. This tray slows a word down until she can count its sounds before she has to choose a single letter.

Set it up

Four things, in the order his hand reaches for them. Everything closed or covered until he needs it — the letter box stays shut for the first few weeks.

  • A small basket with six objects. Six, not forty. He should be able to finish the tray.
  • A dish of ten glass counters, all the same colour — a colour difference invents a rule you did not intend.
  • Two mats, the 3-box on top. He starts with three sounds and stays there for weeks.
  • A lidded box of letters at the back. Closed. It comes out only when he can place counters without help.

The presentation, word for word

Do this once, slowly, with one child, sitting on his right so your hand does not cross his eyes. It takes about four minutes. Use sun the first time: every one of its sounds can be stretched.

  1. Put the mat in front of him, 3-box side up.
  2. Take the sun out of the basket. Stand it above the mat. Say: “This is a sun.”
  3. Slide the dish of counters to his right hand. Take three out and line them up under the mat.
  4. “Watch my mouth.” Stretch the word without breaking it: ssss—uuuu—nnnn, one long breath. Never “suh-uh-nuh.”
  5. Say it stretched again, and this time tap the table once for each sound: three taps, two fingers.
  6. Now the counters. Saying ssss, push one into the first box. uuuu — second. nnnn — third. Then the whole word at normal speed: “sun.”
  7. Take the counters back out and put the sun back. Push the basket to him: “Your turn.” Then stop talking.
  8. Once he can do three objects in a row, open the letter box. Point at the first counter: “What sound is in this box? Find me the s.” He swaps counter for letter, and so on across.
  9. Read it back together, finger under each letter, then again faster: s–u–n. Sun.
  10. Everything goes back on the tray before he chooses another object. That part is the lesson too.

Stretchy and stoppy sounds. You can hold /s/, /m/, /n/, /f/, /l/ and every vowel as long as you like. You cannot hold /p/, /t/, /k/, /g/, /b/, /d/ — they stop. For those, say the sound crisply and tap once instead. Never add a “uh” to make it stretchable: “puh-i-guh” is four sounds and will cost you months.

Object order, continuous sounds first. Introduce the six miniatures in this order across the weeks: sun → mug → hat → bed → pig → cat. Sun leads because every one of its sounds stretches; the later words mix in a stop or two once he has the stretch-and-tap idea.

Working, or not working

It is working when
  • He stretches the word himself before you ask him to.
  • He pauses in the middle of a word to listen, instead of guessing.
  • He notices a leftover box and says the word again without looking at you.
If you see this, do this
  • Pushes all three counters in at once. He is counting, not listening. Cover the last two boxes with your hand and do one sound at a time.
  • Always drops the middle sound. Say only the vowel, long and silly, and have him push one counter for that alone. Then rebuild the word around it.
  • Says letter names — “ess” for /s/. Do not correct him mid-word. Stop, do two minutes of sandpaper letters, come back tomorrow.
  • Grabs letters before pushing counters. Take the letter box off the tray for a week. The counters are doing the teaching.

Variations for the next four weeks

  • Before Week 2, if he is not ready for three sounds: VC warm-ups on the same 3-box mat with the third box covered — at, it, an, in. Two boxes only, both stretchy, so counting never outruns his ear.
  • Week 2: six new objects, same mat. Repetition on a familiar material is not wasted time.
  • Week 3: bring out the second mat, 4-box side up, with tile-tin words — naps → snap → spat → spit → stuck. Naps and snap are the same four tiles rearranged; the rest change one tile at a time.
  • Week 4: picture cards instead of miniatures, so the tray keeps running when the objects wander off.
  • Any week, and they love it: he says the word and you push the counters — and you get one wrong on purpose. He has to catch you. This is the hardest version, because he must hold the word and check yours at the same time.

Why this works

Joseph (2000) taught at-risk kindergartners with word boxes and compared them to children taught the same content without; the box group gained more in hearing sounds, in letter-sound knowledge and in spelling. The reason is the one the explainer page draws: reading collapses letters into a word, but spelling fans one sound out into a choice of letters. A counter carries the fact that a sound happened without carrying any spelling — so the child does the hard half, hearing that there are three separate things in there, before he is asked to decide anything. Swapping counter for letter, one for one, is orthographic mapping (Ehri) slowed down enough to watch.

Move on when — all three

  • He places counters for a four-sound word without you stretching it first.
  • He spots his own leftover box and fixes it, without checking your face.
  • He swaps every counter for a letter and reads the word back — on three different objects in one sitting.

Movable alphabet & object box

“You don’t have to write it. Just build it.”

The object in his hand. He sounds out what he laid down and hears whether it matches. Spelling is not corrected on this tray — that happens on Tray 4.

Why this tray

Montessori (1912); Lillard (2017). Composition with movable letters comes before handwriting because it removes the motor load — the child spells with his hands months before his hand can form the letters.

What problem this tray solves

Leo can push counters and swap them for letters, but only because the mat tells him there are three. Take the boxes away and he stalls: he does not know how many letters to reach for. On this tray the word lives only in his head and the letters are in a heap. It is the first time he spells with nothing holding his place for him — and still no pencil, so he has attention to spare.

Set it up

Three things and a bare mat. What matters here is what is missing: no pencil, no paper, no answer card, nothing on the mat except the object he is working on.

  • An object box of eight miniatures, all spellable with letters he knows.
  • One felt mat, about 40×30 cm. It marks where the work happens and where it stops.
  • The large movable alphabet, open, at his writing hand, vowels where he can see them.

The presentation, word for word

Sit on his right. This presentation is mostly silence — the hunting through the letter box is the work, and if you find letters for him there is no work left.

  1. Unroll the felt mat. Open the alphabet box beside it so the letters face him.
  2. Take the cat out of the box. Stand it at the top-left of the mat. Nothing else goes on the mat.
  3. Ask: “What is it?” He says cat. He has to name it, so the word is his and not yours.
  4. “Say it slowly.” He stretches it. If he cannot, stop and put the sound-box mat back on his tray for a week. No amount of this tray will fix that.
  5. “What’s the first sound? Find that letter.” Then wait. Let him search the whole box. Do not point.
  6. He lays the letter at the left edge of the mat, next to the object.
  7. “Say it again. What comes next?” The second letter goes to the right of the first, touching it.
  8. Same for the last sound. Never say a letter name and never say “no.”
  9. When it is finished: “Read it to me.” He touches each letter and sounds it out. If it reads back wrong he will usually hear it and fix it. If not, leave it.
  10. Letters go back in their compartments; he takes the next object. Never leave a built word out to be admired — the building is the work, not the product.

The thing you must not do. He builds kat. Say nothing. If you cannot bear the silence, say “Yes — that says cat” and move on. He meets the c on Tray 4, on a card, in private — where corrections land without damage. Correct him here and he learns to wait for you before he commits, and a child who waits cannot spell.

Working, or not working

It is working when
  • He reaches for a letter instead of scanning the whole box.
  • He builds left to right without being reminded.
  • He reads his word back and fixes his own letter order.
If you see this, do this
  • Lays letters right to left, or scatters them. Put a small pencil mark at the left edge of the mat. Say “start here.” Say nothing else, ever again.
  • Builds ct — no vowel. He is not hearing the middle. Back to Tray 1 for a week, with this same object.
  • Asks “is that right?” Answer only with “Read it to me and see.” Never answer it yourself, not once.
  • Takes twenty minutes over one word. Too many objects and too big a box. Cut it to four objects for a fortnight.

Variations for the next four weeks

  • Week 2: same objects, but he draws one out of the box without looking. Removing the choice speeds everything up.
  • Week 3: two objects at once, built one above the other, so he can see the letters they share.
  • Week 4: no objects at all — you whisper a word to him and he builds it. This is dictation without a pencil, and it is the bridge to Tray 4.
  • Any week: he builds his own name, then a friend’s. The name is the word every child wants first, and it buys you a fortnight of enthusiasm.

Why this works

Montessori (1912) put composing before handwriting for a mechanical reason that has since been measured. Berninger et al. (2002) showed that in young writers it is transcription — forming the letters, recalling the spellings — that limits how much gets written, not ideas. While a five-year-old’s hand is busy drawing an a, the rest of the word leaks out of his head; taking the pencil out of the loop hands his whole attention back to the spelling choice. Lillard (2017) collects the classroom evidence. And this is where the explainer’s rule bites hardest: invented spelling is welcome here precisely so he keeps making choices instead of waiting for permission.

Word chains

On the tray

  3. Reads the new word aloud before moving on.

Why this tray

McCandliss, Beck, Sandak & Perfetti (2003). A minimal-pair “word building” chain — changing one letter at a time — produced measurable decoding gains in struggling readers over ordinary phonics practice.

What problem this tray solves

Sara can build mop. Ask her for hop and she sweeps the whole thing away and starts again from nothing. That tells you she is holding mop as one memorised lump, not as three parts she can reach into. Spelling a word she has never seen needs the parts to come out separately, one at a time — and this is the only tray that makes her do exactly that.

Set it up

A board, a tin, and a small stack of cards. The board matters: fixed slots stop him from rebuilding, because the letters that are not changing physically stay where they are.

  • Six chain cards in a stack, picture side up, so he can see where he is going.
  • The chain board flat in front of him, four slots running left to right.
  • A tin of 30 letter tiles — doubles of all fifteen. A beginner only gets the one chain’s letters out at a time — six or seven tiles, not the whole tin.
  • Nothing to write with. This tray is hands only.

The presentation, word for word

The whole skill lives in how you say the two words. Say them close together, in one breath, twice. A gap between them lets him forget the first one, and then he rebuilds.

  1. Take the top chain card and show him the picture. “Mop.”
  2. Build mop in the slots yourself, saying each sound as the tile lands: /m/ /o/ /p/.
  3. Push the board to him. “Read it.” He reads mop.
  4. Now the move: “Now make hop.” Then say the pair back to back, close together, twice: mop … hop. mop … hop.
  5. Wait. Do not point at the slot that changes. The waiting is the whole lesson.
  6. When he lifts a tile, say nothing until it lands. Then: “Read it.” He reads hop.
  7. “Now make hot.” Same rhythm: hop … hot. hop … hot.
  8. Work through five words. If he stalls, say the pair with the differing part stretched and everything else quiet: mmmm–op … hhhh–op.
  9. Turn the card over. He checks the chain against the back himself.

The presentation, word for word

The step everyone skips is step 7. Breaking a sentence on purpose is what teaches a child that a sentence can be wrong, which is what makes reading it back worth doing.

  1. Lay the sentence line in front of him, running left to right.
  2. Open the tin and show him the three compartments: “These are things. These are what they do. These are the little words that hold them together.”
  3. Take the, pig, is, big. Lay them down one at a time, saying each word out loud as it lands.

  9. When he has one he is pleased with, give him a strip and the pencil: “Now write it down.” He copies from his own cards, which are right there, so nothing has to be remembered.
  10. Last, every time, until the question stops being needed: “Where’s your dot?”

Lay it out

Left to right: one small dish of all three symbol types, his six sentence strips from Tray 5, the symbol key card, then the four miniatures for the oral game at the back.

Do this, step by step

  1. First, for weeks, with no tray at all: put four objects on a mat, say “Bring me the pig.” He fetches it — explain nothing.
  2. Change it: “RUN!” He runs. Then “JUMP!” and “SIT!” — still explain nothing.
  3. Mix them: “Bring me the cat — now HOP with the cat!” Play for weeks, lining up, tidying, until boring.
  4. Only then, bring out the tray. Put his own strip in front of him: the pig is big.
  5. Ask: “Which word is the thing?” He points at pig.
  6. Hand him the black triangle: “This shape means a thing.” He places it above pig.
  7. On a doing-word strip — the dog runs — give him the red circle: “Red, because it moves.” The whole explanation.
  8. The dark-blue describing triangle, for a word like big, comes weeks later.
  9. He checks his own placement against the symbol key.
  10. Never more than two symbols per session — three shapes is the whole set for now.
Then the child works alone.
  • He plays the oral fetch-and-do game with a friend, no tray involved.
  • He reads his own strip, places symbols, checks against the symbol key.
  • He sorts the symbols back into the dish before choosing another strip.

Why it works

Montessori (1918) taught word function as a physical command game before any written exercise, since a five-year-old learns a grammatical category by obeying it, not defining it. Cain (2007) found grammatical awareness in young children predicts later reading comprehension. See the Philosophy page for why this comes last, after his own sentence on Tray 5.

Move him on when

  • He names a word’s job for a word that was never on a card.
  • He finds the naming word in a sentence he has not seen before.
  • He moves a symbol he has placed wrongly before he checks the symbol key.

04 · The daily loop

Fifteen minutes, every day, in the same order

The shelf is for individual work. This loop is the small-group time that keeps it moving. Same four moves, same order, every day — the children should be able to run it without you.

05 · Moving on

One sign per tray. Not a calendar, a signal.

You do not move a child on because it is week six. You move him when you see one specific thing — the sign the tray has finished its job.

  • 01Sound boxes → 2. He places the counters for a four-sound word without you saying it slowly first.
  • 02Movable alphabet → 3. He builds a word you never named — he picked the object and did it alone.
  • 03Word chains → 4. He changes the one letter straight away, without rebuilding the whole word.
  • 04Dictation → 5. Three cards in a row where his first attempt already matches the back.
  • 05Sentence builder → 6. He puts the full stop on without being told, on a sentence he made up himself.
  • 06Story books → 7. He fills a fold-book to the last page without once asking how to spell anything.
  • 07Author’s chair → 8. He asks to tell a story instead of waiting to be asked.
  • 08Grammar symbols → the next shelf. He says “pig is a naming word” about a word that was never on a card.
If a child stalls

Go back one tray, not forward. A child stuck on dictation almost always needs Tray 1 again with a four-sound word — he is guessing at letters because he is not yet hearing all the sounds. The shelf is a ladder, and the rung below is always the fix.

The rule, if you only keep one line

Every tray moves a sound out of the child’s mouth, onto the table, then onto paper. Spelling is celebrated on Trays 2, 6 and 7 and corrected only on Tray 4. Fifteen minutes daily: 3 on trays 1–3, 4 on tray 4, 4 on tray 5, 4 on trays 6–7.
