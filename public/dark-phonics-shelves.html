The children can read the cat. Now build the

shelf.

Eight trays, two shelves, left to right. Nothing else. This is what goes on the language shelf after CVC decoding, in the order a child meets it, with the exact contents of every tray.

8 trays · 12 pictures · about ten minutes

The one rule

Every tray moves a sound out of the child’s mouth, onto the table, then onto paper.

That is the whole shelf. If a tray does not do one of those three moves, it does not belong here — put it on the reading shelf instead.

Reading collapses letters into a word. Encoding runs the other way: the child starts with a sound stuck in his own head and has to get it out. It leaves his mouth, becomes something he can push around with his hands, and only then becomes a pencil mark. Skip the middle move and you get a child who reads fluently and freezes at a blank page.

01 · The shelf map

Two shelves. Four trays each. That is the whole language area.

Left to right is hardest-last. A child works across the top shelf, then across the one below, and never chooses from the far right until he has been shown it.

Eight is a ceiling, not a target. If you can only get six made, run six — a shelf with two half-built trays teaches the children that materials are optional.

Two decisions I made for you

There is no separate phonogram tray. Heart words and the first phonograms live on a small ring inside Tray 4, because dictation is where a child discovers that some words cannot be sounded out. And grammar comes last, on the smallest tray, played out loud before it is ever a written exercise.

02 · The eight trays

What is on each tray, exactly

Counts are real counts. Six objects means six, not a basket of forty. A tray a child can finish is a tray a child will choose.

01

Sound boxes

say it · push it · letter it
On the tray
  • 2 mats: one 3-box, one 4-box
  • 10 glass counters in a small dish
  • 6 miniatures from an object box (pig, cat, sun, bed, mug, hat)
  • 1 lidded box of letters: s a t p i n m d g o c k e u
The child does
  1. Takes an object and says its name slowly.
  2. Pushes one counter into one box for each sound.
  3. Swaps every counter for a letter.
You say, once

“Watch my mouth. /p/ … /i/ … /g/. Three sounds, three boxes.”

Control of error

The number of boxes matches the number of sounds. A leftover box, or a counter with nowhere to go, tells him to say the word again — you do not have to.

Have · Buy · Make
  • HaveObject boxes, movable alphabet
  • BuyGlass counters or flat beads, one bag
  • MakeSound-box mats. A4 landscape card, 3-box and 4-box versions, 55 mm squares with 6 mm gutters, heavy outline, no art. Print 2-up, laminate.
Why this tray

Joseph (2000). At-risk kindergartners taught with word boxes gained more in phoneme awareness, letter-sound knowledge and spelling than children taught the same content without them.

02

Movable alphabet & object box

build the word, don’t write it
On the tray
  • 1 large movable alphabet (in its own box, beside the tray)
  • 1 object box, 8 miniatures, all decodable in your SATPIN order
  • 1 felt work mat, about 40×30 cm
  • Nothing else. No pencil.
The child does
  1. Puts one object at the top of the mat.
  2. Says the word slowly and lays the letters left to right.
  3. Reads his word back, touching each letter.
You say, once

“You don’t have to write it. Just build it.”

Control of error

The object in his hand. He sounds out what he laid down and hears whether it matches. Spelling is not corrected on this tray — that happens on Tray 4.

Have · Buy · Make
  • HaveMovable alphabet, object boxes, sandpaper letters (beside it, for a stuck letter)
  • Buy1 felt mat
  • MakeNothing.
Why this tray

Montessori (1912); Lillard (2017). Composition with movable letters comes before handwriting because it removes the motor load — the child spells with his hands months before his hand can form the letters.

03

Word chains

change exactly one sound
On the tray
  • 1 chain board: a strip with 3 letter slots and one 4th slot
  • 1 tin of letter tiles, doubles of s a t p i n m d g o c k
  • 6 chain cards (front: start word + picture; back: the full chain)
The child does
  1. Builds the first word on the board.
  2. Hears the next word and changes one letter.
  3. Reads the new word aloud before moving on.
You say, once

“You made cat. Now make cot. Change only one.”

Control of error

The back of the chain card lists the chain in order. And the rule itself is a control: if more than one tile moved, something went wrong.

Have · Buy · Make
  • HaveLetter tiles / word cards from the build-it sheets
  • MakeChain board. A4 landscape card, four 60×70 mm recessed slots, matt laminate.
  • Make6 chain cards. A6. Front: start word in stroke font + one photo. Back: the 5-word chain, one per line, changed letter in the accent colour. Duplex, flip short edge.
Why this tray

McCandliss, Beck, Sandak & Perfetti (2003). A minimal-pair “word building” chain — changing one letter at a time — produced measurable decoding gains in struggling readers over ordinary phonics practice.

04

Dictation

write it · flip it · fix it yourself
On the tray
  • 12 flip cards (front: photo; back: the word)
  • 1 small notebook per child, in a labelled box
  • 2 sharpened pencils in a pot
  • 1 heart-word ring: a, an, I, the, ate
The child does
  1. Looks at the picture and says the word.
  2. Writes it in his notebook — his own way.
  3. Turns the card over, compares, and rewrites it once underneath.
You say, once

“Write it your way first. Then turn it over and see.”

Control of error

The printed word on the back, in the same stroke font he traces. He never has to ask you, and you never have to mark a book.

Have · Buy · Make
  • HaveWord cards, photo bank, tracing workbooks (the stroke font)
  • BuySmall notebooks, one per child; pencils
  • Make12 dictation flip cards. A6, photo front, word on the back in stroke font at 48 pt, duplex flip on short edge, laminated.
  • MakeHeart-word ring cards. 5 small cards, 60×35 mm, hole-punched, word only, no picture.
Why this tray

Graham & Santangelo (2014). A meta-analysis of spelling instruction found it reliably improves spelling and transfers to word reading — encoding practice pays reading dividends, not just writing ones.

05

Sentence builder

lay it out, then copy it down
On the tray
  • 1 tin of word cards from the readers (about 40, sorted: naming / doing / small words)
  • 1 heart-word ring (same set as Tray 4)
  • 3 punctuation tiles: full stop, question mark, exclamation mark
  • 10 blank lined sentence strips + 1 pencil
The child does
  1. Lays a sentence along the sentence line and ends it with a tile.
  2. Reads it aloud, finger under each word.
  3. Copies it onto a strip and puts his own full stop on.
You say, once

“A sentence tells you who did what. Read me yours.”

Control of error

Reading it out loud. If it does not make sense, it is not a sentence, and he hears that himself. Every strip must end with a mark — a strip with no mark goes back.

Have · Buy · Make
  • HaveWord cards, sentence line, build-it sheets, sentence strips from the tracing pipeline
  • Make3 punctuation tiles. 34×42 mm card, one mark each, oversized, laminated.
  • MakeBlank lined sentence strips. 200×60 mm, three guides with a dashed midline matched to the stroke font’s x-height. Print 4-up on A4.
Why this tray

Berninger et al. (2002). In young writers, transcription — handwriting and spelling — is what limits how much a child can compose. Separating the two lifts the ceiling on both.

06

Story books

four pictures, one blank book, no help
On the tray
  • 4 wordless picture-sequence sets, 4 frames each, in envelopes
  • 6 blank fold-books (one A4 sheet folded to 8 pages)
  • 1 pot of pencils and one of coloured pencils
  • 1 small heart-word card propped up at the back
The child does
  1. Puts the four pictures in an order that tells a story.
  2. Writes one line under each picture in his book.
  3. Draws the cover and writes his name on it.
You say, once

“Spell it the way it sounds. I want your story, not perfect letters.”

Control of error

Only the picture order — it either tells a story when he reads it back or it does not. There is deliberately no spelling control here.

Have · Buy · Make
  • HaveReader art and the photo bank — the source images for the sequences
  • BuyPencils, coloured pencils
  • Make4 picture-sequence sets. 16 cards, 90×90 mm, one scene each, no words, no numbers on the front; set letter on the back so sets do not mix.
  • MakeBlank fold-book template. A4, single sheet, one cut and three folds to 8 pages; each inside page a picture box over two guide lines; front cover has a name line.
Why this tray

Ouellette & Sénéchal (2017). Kindergartners encouraged to invent their own spellings read and spelled better a year later than matched children who were not — guessing at spellings is a route into literacy, not a detour.

07

Author’s chair

he talks · you write · the class acts it
On the tray
  • 1 folder with 20 story sheets (lined top half, blank bottom half)
  • 1 clipboard with the class list and a tick column
  • 1 pen for you, pencils for him
  • 1 chair, kept for this and nothing else
The child does
  1. Tells you the story; you write it word for word.
  2. Draws his picture underneath.
  3. Sits in the chair and casts the class to act it out.
You say, once

“Tell me the story. I’ll write down exactly what you say.”

Control of error

The audience. If the class can act it, the story made sense. Nothing is marked, nothing is corrected, and the child’s words are not improved on the way down.

Have · Buy · Make
  • HaveA chair, a folder, the class list
  • MakeStory-dictation sheet. A4 portrait. Name and date line, 6 wide ruled lines for your handwriting, a 120 mm blank box below for his drawing, and a small “acted on ___” line in the corner.
  • MakeTeacher script card. A5, 6 lines: how to take a story, what never to change, how to run the acting in 4 minutes.
Why this tray

Nicolopoulou et al. (2015); Cooper (2005). A storytelling-and-story-acting programme run in preschools produced gains in narrative skill, vocabulary and print knowledge over matched control classrooms.

08

Grammar symbols

a game first, a material second
On the tray
  • 1 dish of symbols: 6 black triangles (naming), 6 red circles (doing), 6 small dark-blue triangles (describing)
  • 6 sentence strips the children built on Tray 5
  • 6 matching control cards with the symbols already placed
  • 4 miniatures for the oral game
The child does
  1. Reads a sentence he built himself.
  2. Puts a black triangle on the naming word.
  3. Puts a red circle on the doing word, then checks the control card.
You say, once — and out loud, weeks before the tray appears

“Bring me the pig. … Now — run with the pig!”

Control of error

The control card underneath, showing the same sentence with its symbols. He lifts it to check, not you.

Have · Buy · Make
  • HaveObject-box miniatures, sentence strips from Tray 5
  • MakeGrammar symbol tokens. Printed card, cut and laminated: black triangle 35 mm base, red circle 30 mm, dark-blue triangle 25 mm. 6 of each on one A4.
  • Make6 control cards. 200×60 mm, sentence in stroke font with the symbols printed above the right words.
  • BuyWooden symbols — later, only if the card ones get loved to death
Why this tray

Montessori (1918); Cain (2007). Montessori taught function of words as a physical command game long before a written one; grammatical awareness measured in young children predicts later reading comprehension.

What is deliberately not here

No blue-series blends tray, no green-series phonogram folders, no reading-command boxes. Those are the next shelf, and they are reading work, not encoding work. Adding them now is exactly the fuzziness you are trying to get rid of — eight trays that all get used beat fourteen that half do.

03 · The daily loop

Fifteen minutes, every day, in the same order

The shelf is for individual work. This loop is the small-group time that keeps it moving. Same four moves, same order, every day — the children should be able to run it without you.

04 · Moving on

One sign per tray. Not a calendar, a signal.

You do not move a child on because it is week six. You move him when you see one specific thing — the sign the tray has finished its job.

  • 01Sound boxes → 2. He places the counters for a four-sound word without you saying it slowly first.
  • 02Movable alphabet → 3. He builds a word you never named — he picked the object and did it alone.
  • 03Word chains → 4. He changes the one letter straight away, without rebuilding the whole word.
  • 04Dictation → 5. Three cards in a row where his first attempt already matches the back.
  • 05Sentence builder → 6. He puts the full stop on without being told, on a sentence he made up himself.
  • 06Story books → 7. He fills a fold-book to the last page without once asking how to spell anything.
  • 07Author’s chair → 8. He asks to tell a story instead of waiting to be asked.
  • 08Grammar symbols → the next shelf. He says “pig is a naming word” about a word that was never on a card.
If a child stalls

Go back one tray, not forward. A child stuck on dictation almost always needs Tray 1 again with a four-sound word — he is guessing at letters because he is not yet hearing all the sounds. The shelf is a ladder, and the rung below is always the fix.

The rule, if you only keep one line

Every tray moves a sound out of the child’s mouth, onto the table, then onto paper. Spelling is celebrated on Trays 2, 6 and 7 and corrected only on Tray 4. Fifteen minutes daily: 3 on trays 1–3, 4 on tray 4, 4 on tray 5, 4 on trays 6–7.
