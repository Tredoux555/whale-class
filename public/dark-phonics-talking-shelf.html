The children can write the cat. Now make them

talk.

Four trays beside the Writing Shelf. Three that check themselves and one that cannot. This is what goes on the language shelf for a child who reads and writes English and does not yet speak it, in the order he meets it, with the exact contents of every tray.

4 trays · 6 printables · about ten minutes

The one rule

The words must do something.

On this shelf a child’s sentence moves an object, orders a picture or answers a puppet — and the tray shows him whether it worked. Nobody marks his speaking. The mat does.

That is the whole shelf, and it is the thing the Writing Shelf could not do. A pencil mark sits still and can be checked at leisure; a spoken sentence is gone the second it leaves his mouth, and the only way to know whether it landed is to give it something to move. So every work here hands the sentence a job. If a tray does not give the words something to do, it does not belong here — it is a conversation, and conversations belong on Tray 4 with you.

  • 01A frame card stands at the back of every tray. The sentence is given; the child fills the gap. He is never asked to generate from nothing.
  • 02Every work has a silent way in. Point, build, order — before any words. A child in his silent period can do this shelf on the day he arrives.
  • 03Every word is one he already owns from the Writing Shelf. The word tin, the heart-word ring, the current letter. This is not where new vocabulary is taught.
  • 04The shelf is a ladder. One word → one frame sentence → four sentences in order → a two-line exchange.

01 · The shelf map

Four trays. Three check themselves, one needs you.

Left to right, easiest first. Trays 1 to 3 are shelf works a child chooses on his own or with one other child. Tray 4 is a routine, and it is on the map because it is where the shelf is going.

Say this out loud when someone asks what Tray 4 is

“It is a routine, not a shelf work.” Every other tray on this shelf tells the child whether his sentence worked. Tray 4 deliberately does not, because a conversation is the thing all three of the others are training for, and a conversation needs a person. Shelve it with the teacher card face up so nobody mistakes it for a work a child finishes alone.

02 · The four trays

What is on each tray, exactly

Counts are real counts. Eight objects means eight, twice over — one set each, or there is nothing to compare. A tray a child can finish is a tray a child will choose.

01

Behind the Screen

he can’t see · tell him · lift the screen
On the tray
  • 2 identical felt mats, about 30 × 20 cm.
  • 2 identical sets of 8 miniatures — actors cat, pig, dog, nut; places bed, pot, tin, hat. The first set comes off the object boxes; the second is bought.
  • 1 folding card screen, about 30 × 25 cm — buy one, or fold an A4 card.
  • 1 frame card at the back. Print PDF
  • 8 arrangement cards in a box. Print PDF
The child does
  1. A hides his mat behind the screen, sets 2–4 objects and tells B, one sentence per object.
  2. B builds what he hears on his own mat.
  3. Lift the screen and compare.

Solo: copy an arrangement card, then say it to the card.

Control of error

The two mats match or they don’t — both children see which object is wrong.

The frame card says
  • The ___ is ON the ___.
  • The ___ is IN the ___.
  • The ___ is NEXT TO the ___.
You say once

“He can’t see. Tell him.”

Move on when

A pair completes a four-object round with no pointing, twice.

What problem this tray solves

Mia has been in the classroom five months. She reads the cat is on the mat off a card without hesitating, and she writes it. Ask her, in English, where the cat is, and nothing comes out. Not because she does not know — because a spoken sentence has never once had to do anything, so there has never been a reason to finish one. This tray gives her a reason: there is a child on the other side of the screen holding the same cat, and he cannot see.

Why this works for a Mandarin-L1 child

Two things are going on, and they matter in this order.

The mat checks the speech, not the adult. A four-year-old learning her second language is doing something socially enormous every time she opens her mouth, and an adult listening for errors is the fastest way to close it again. The barrier game removes the adult from the loop entirely: when the screen comes down, the cat is on the bed or it is in the pot, and the information is in the objects. Nobody was right or wrong out loud. The Bell Foundation’s EAL guidance on barrier games makes exactly this point — two people each hold what the other lacks, the task can only be finished by talking, and the completion itself is the feedback.

The silent period is real, and this tray does not exclude her. A young child newly immersed in a second language may produce almost nothing for weeks or months while she absorbs input, and pushing her to speak early can backfire — anxiety, shutdown, occasionally mistaken for something clinical. So the work has a way in with no words at all: she takes an arrangement card and copies it onto her mat. Placing four objects correctly is a legitimate pass through this material. She will speak when the reason to speak is stronger than the reason not to, and a child waiting on the other side of a screen is a strong reason.

The third thing is smaller but it is why the words come out at all: she is never asked to invent a sentence. The frame card is standing there with three sentences on it and a gap in each. Mandarin does not mark prepositions the way English does, and on versus in is a real difficulty and not carelessness — so the frame puts the hard word in capitals, in the middle, and lets her fill the two easy ends.

Set it up

Five things, and the counts are not suggestions. Two mats, two identical sets of eight, one screen, one frame card, one box of cards.

  • The mats are identical. A bigger mat on one side is a different problem to solve.
  • The two object sets are identical, eight each. This is the item most people skip and it is the item the tray cannot run without.
  • The screen stands on its own. A book held up by a teacher is a teacher in the loop.
  • The frame card leans at the back where both children can read it without picking it up.
  • The arrangement cards live in a box, dots facing up, so a child can pick his own level.

Working, or not working

It is working when
  • He gives one sentence per object and stops, instead of describing everything at once.
  • She asks him to say it again, out loud, in English.
  • They lift the screen and neither of them looks at you.
If you see this, do this
  • He points over the screen. Say the one line — “He can’t see. Tell him.” — and then say nothing more, ever, that round.
  • She builds nothing. Drop to two objects and one-dot cards for a week. Four objects is three sentences too many.
  • He uses on for everything. Sit the two sentences side by side on the frame card and put the cat physically on and then in the pot, saying nothing. Do not drill it.
  • Neither of them speaks at all. Both take an arrangement card and work solo, side by side, for a fortnight. The pair work is not going anywhere.

Move on when

  • A pair completes a four-object round with no pointing, twice.

In one breath

Two children sit either side of a screen with the same eight objects. One arranges his and tells the other where each one goes; the other builds what he hears; then the screen comes down and they see whether the two mats match.

The first fifteen minutes, step by step

Do this once, with two children, at a table where nobody walks past. It takes about fifteen minutes and you will talk for about ninety seconds of it.

  1. Put the two mats down facing each other. Stand the screen between them so neither child can see the other’s mat.
  2. Give each child the same eight objects, in the same order, left to right. Say nothing about which ones to use.
  3. Stand the frame card at the back, where both of them can read it. Read the three lines out once, pointing: “The ___ is ON the ___. The ___ is IN the ___. The ___ is NEXT TO the ___.”
  4. Say to A, quietly: “Put two things on your mat. Don’t let him see.” Two, not four.
  5. Then, to both of them, once: “He can’t see. Tell him.” That is the whole presentation. Now stop talking.
  6. A says a sentence. B moves an object. If B moves nothing, wait. If A points over the screen, put your hand flat on the screen and say nothing.
  7. When A has finished both objects, lift the screen yourself, slowly, so they both watch the same moment.
  8. They compare. Say nothing at all — not “well done”, not “nearly”. If an object is in the wrong place they will find it, and finding it is the lesson.
  9. Swap the roles without discussion: the teller becomes the builder. Do it once more, still two objects.
  10. Put everything back on the tray, objects in their sets, screen folded, cards square. Then leave.

Never present a four-object round first. Four objects is three sentences a child has to hold, and a pair who fail the first round will not choose the tray again. Two objects, twice, is a complete first presentation.

The level dots. Cards 1–4 carry one dot, 5–6 two, 7–8 three. It is a level, not an order — he may take any one-dot card in any order, and the dots are what stop him taking a four-object card in week one.

Where the words come from

Every word on this tray is one he already owns: the eight objects are the object-box miniatures from Writing Shelf Trays 1 and 2, and the sentence shape is the one he has built with cards on Writing Shelf Tray 5. This is not where new vocabulary is taught. It is where the vocabulary he already has becomes spoken sentences.

02

Tell It, Order It

tell it · don’t show it · open both
On the tray
  • The three Tray-6 picture-sequence sets printed TWICE — six envelopes, A A B B C C. That is Writing Shelf sheet 06; do not rebuild it. Print PDF ×2
  • 1 frame card. Print PDF
  • 1 story card per set — 3 cards, 70 × 70 mm printed and 90 × 90 mounted, text only. Front: set letter big. Back: the four lines. Print PDF
The child does
  1. A takes one envelope and tells the story one picture at a time — “First… Then… Next… Last.”
  2. B lays his copy of the same set in the order he hears.
  3. Open both and compare.

Solo: order a set, then tell it to the puppet from Tray 3.

Control of error

The two rows match.

The frame card says
  • FIRST…
  • THEN…
  • NEXT…
  • LAST…
You say once

“Tell it — don’t show it.”

Move on when

B orders all four from A’s telling alone, and A uses First and Last without the card.

The three story cards
  • A. The seed is in the pot. / The sprout is up. / The leaves are big. / The sunflower is out!
  • B. The hen sat on the egg. / The egg has a crack. / The chick is out. / The hen and the chick go.
  • C. The mouse has an apple. / The mouse has a bite. / The apple is half. / The apple is a core!

What problem this tray solves

Tray 1 gets one sentence out of him at a time, and each sentence is independent: if he gets the second one wrong, the first one still stood. A story is not like that. Four events have to come out of his mouth in an order, and each one has to be understood before the next arrives, because B is building a row and cannot go back. This is the first tray where his talking has to hold together across more than one sentence.

Why this works for a Mandarin-L1 child

The material checks the sequence, not the grammar. B’s row is right or it is wrong, and it went wrong at a particular picture, which both children can point at. Nothing about A’s tense, article or word order is inspected by anybody — and that is what lets a child who is still getting all three of those wrong tell a whole story out loud. He is being judged on whether he was understood, which is the only thing that matters at four.

The four ordering words are a formulaic frame, and that is a feature. First, then, next, last are the joints of every narrative he will ever tell. Handing them to him printed, in order, is exactly the sentence-frame move the EAL literature recommends: repeated use of a fixed frame is how the frame stops being scaffolding and becomes grammar he owns. He is not inventing structure; he is filling four slots. The invention is in what goes in them.

The silent way in is the ordering itself. A child who will not tell can still lay a set out in the right order, alone, and that is a complete pass through the work. Ordering four pictures is a real cognitive job — it is what Writing Shelf Tray 6 asks before the writing starts — and doing it silently for a fortnight is not stalling.

Set it up

Six envelopes, and they are three sets twice over. Label them on the back, never on the cards: A A B B C C.

  • Hand out matching envelopes. Two different sets is not this work.
  • The frame card stands at the back so A can see the four words without holding anything.
  • The three story cards live with the envelopes, letter side up. They are the check, afterwards — not what the story is told from.

Working, or not working

It is working when
  • He says four things, not one long thing.
  • She lays a picture down and waits, instead of laying all four.
  • They open both rows and start comparing before either of them looks up.
If you see this, do this
  • He turns the picture round. “Tell it — don’t show it.” Once, and then nothing.
  • He tells all four at once and she is lost. Put your hand over the three he has not told yet. He tells one, she lays one.
  • She lays the whole row instantly, from memory. Good problem: she has done this set before. Swap sets.
  • Neither will tell. Both work solo — order a set, tell it to the Tray 3 puppet. That is the whole work, done alone, and it counts.

Move on when

  • B orders all four from A’s telling alone, and A uses First and Last without the card.

In one breath

One child tells a four-picture story out loud, one picture at a time, while the other lays out his own copy of the same pictures in the order he hears. Then they open both rows and compare.

The first fifteen minutes, step by step

  1. Choose set A — seed to sunflower. It is the clearest of the three and nothing in it needs explaining.
  2. Give A one envelope marked A and B the other envelope marked A. Say: “You have the same pictures. He can’t see yours.”
  3. A tips his four out where B cannot see them and puts them in order himself. Let him take as long as he likes; do not help.
  4. Point at the frame card and read it once: “First… Then… Next… Last.”
  5. Say the one line: “Tell it — don’t show it.” Then stop talking for the rest of the presentation.
  6. A says the first line. B finds the picture he thinks it is and puts it down on the left. He does not get to ask which one it is.
  7. Repeat for all four. If A stalls, point at the next word on the frame card. Do not supply the sentence.
  8. Lay the two rows side by side, face up, and let them look. Say nothing at all.
  9. Turn the story card over and read the four lines with them, once: that is the check, and it is where the language they were reaching for is written down.
  10. Swap envelopes and swap roles. Everything back in its own envelope before the tray goes back.

The story card is not the script. He tells it off the pictures, in his own words. The card is what he compares against afterwards and what a solo child says the story to. If it becomes the thing he reads aloud, put it back in the box for a week.

Puppet Talk

the cat asks · the potato answers · turn it over
On the tray

  • 8 say-it cards — 80 × 120 mm printed, 100 × 140 mounted. Picture front, exchange back, printed BIG in two lines: question, then answer. Print PDF
  • 1 frame card. Print PDF
The child does
  1. Picks a card and a puppet.
  2. The cat asks the question, the potato answers — the child does both voices, or two children one each.
  3. Turns the card to check.

Silent entry: hold the puppet and act the answer.

Control of error

The printed lines — every word is a reader word, he can read them.

The frame card says
  • WHO is it?
  • WHAT can it do?
  • WHERE is it?
  • IS it ___?
You say once

“The puppet says what the card says.”

Move on when

He says a line without looking at the card, and adds one of his own.

The eight say-it cards
  • 1. “Who is it?” / “It is a cat.” · 2. “Who is it?” / “It is a pig.”
  • 3. “What can it do?” / “It can sit.” · 4. “What can it do?” / “It can jump.”
  • 5. “Where is it?” / “It is on the mat.” · 6. “Where is it?” / “It is in the box.”
  • 7. “Is it big?” / “Yes, it is big.” · 8. “Is it wet?” / “No, it is not wet.”

What problem this tray solves

Trays 1 and 2 both need another child. This one does not, and that matters more than it sounds: a child who will not yet speak in front of a peer will speak in front of nobody, holding a puppet, and a child whose partner is away can still do the work. It is also the first tray where he has to answer a question rather than describe something — which is the shape a conversation actually has.

Why this works for a Mandarin-L1 child

The printed line is a recast with no adult in it. The long-standing advice in child second-language work is to recast — repeat what the child said back in corrected form without announcing an error — because overt correction shuts production down while a recast keeps the conversation going and still supplies the right form. A card that carries the exchange on its back does exactly that, and it does it one second after he spoke, in private, with nobody watching his face. He says it, he turns the card, he reads what it should have been. Nothing is announced.

A puppet moves the speaking off him. This is not a charming detail; it is why the tray works for the child who has stopped talking. The sentence belongs to the cat. If the cat says it wrong, the cat said it wrong.

Every word is a reader word. That is the whole control of error. If a single word on the back of a card were one he could not decode, the card would stop checking him and start needing you. The eight exchanges ladder on purpose: who (1–2), what it can do (3–4), where (5–6), then a yes/no question with a yes answer and a no answer (7–8), because negation in English is a separate thing to learn and it is easiest to meet beside its own positive.

The silent entry is acting. A child holds the puppet and acts the answer — the puppet sits, the puppet jumps. He has done the work, and he has done it with the question in his ears.

Set it up

  • Two puppets, flat on the tray, not in a pot — a pot of puppets becomes a toy.
  • Eight cards in a stand or a small box, picture side up.
  • The frame card at the back, so the four question words are always visible even when he has not chosen a card yet.

Working, or not working

It is working when
  • He uses two different voices without being asked to.
  • He turns the card over himself, before saying anything to you.
  • He makes up a ninth card out loud.
If you see this, do this
  • He reads the back first. Turn the stand round and hand him one card at a time, picture up.
  • He answers but will not ask. Take the cat yourself for two rounds and ask the question; he answers with the potato. Then hand the cat back.
  • He plays with the puppets and never speaks. Leave it. Acting the answer is the silent entry, and it is a pass through the work.
  • You want to correct his pronunciation. Do not. The card has already said what the card says.

Move on when

  • He says a line without looking at the card, and adds one of his own.

In one breath

The child takes a card and two stick puppets. The cat asks the question printed on the card, the potato answers it, and then he turns the card over and reads the two lines to check himself.

The first fifteen minutes, step by step

  1. Sit beside him, not opposite. Put both puppets flat between you.
  2. Take card 1 off the stand and put it down picture up. Say nothing about the picture — he knows it, it is a page out of a reader he has read.
  3. Pick up the cat. In a cat’s voice, ask: “Who is it?”
  4. Pick up the potato with your other hand. In a different voice, answer: “It is a cat.”
  5. Turn the card over so he sees the two lines printed there. Read them with him, finger under each. Then turn it back.
  6. Hand him both puppets. Say the one line: “The puppet says what the card says.” Then stop talking.
  7. He does card 1. If he says only the answer, that is a pass. If he says nothing and makes the potato nod, that is also a pass.
  8. Let him take cards 2, 3 and 4 himself. Four cards is a whole session; do not go to eight on day one.
  9. Never correct the sound of a word on this tray. If he says it differently from you, turn the card over and read it together, and say nothing else.
  10. Cards square on the stand, puppets flat on the tray, before he chooses another work.

The cat and the potato are not random. They are the Dark Phonics cast the children already know off the readers and the song cards — so a child picking up the cat is picking up somebody, not a shape.

When he adds one of his own. That is the sign for this tray, and it usually arrives as a joke. Write his line on a blank card and put it in the box. It is now card 9.

04

Tell Me About It

a routine, not a shelf work
This one is a routine, not a shelf work

Every other tray on this shelf tells the child whether his sentence worked. This one does not, and it is not meant to — it is the bridge to conversation, and it needs you. It sits on the shelf the way the Writing Shelf’s author’s chair does: a child brings it to you, you do not summon him.

On the tray
  • A card stand with one photo prompt — reuse the Tray-4 dictation photos, Writing Shelf sheet 03. Print PDF
  • 1 teacher card, A5 2-up like sheet 09. Print PDF
The child does

Picks a photo, tells you about it, hears it said back.

You say once

“Tell me about it.”

The teacher card says
  • Tell me about it.
  • Say it once, then wait.
  • Ask one thing only: “What else?”
  • Say his sentence back to him, correct, as if you agree. Never say no.
  • Stop when he stops.
Control of error

None by design — this is the bridge to conversation, and it needs you.

Move on when

He adds a second sentence before you ask.

What problem this tray solves

Trays 1 to 3 all hand him a sentence to fill. That is what makes them work, and it is also their ceiling: nothing he says on those trays is his. A conversation has no frame card in it. So there has to be one place where the frame comes off — and the moment it does, the material can no longer check anything, because there is no right answer to check against. That is not a flaw in the design. It is the design.

Why this works for a Mandarin-L1 child

It is dialogic reading, with the book taken away. The best-tested oral-language intervention for young children is the adult routine of prompting, evaluating, expanding and repeating — and it has been trialled specifically with additional-language learners in preschool, with gains over control classrooms. It is adult-led, so it is not a shelf work and this page does not pretend it is. But the mechanism is exactly what the four printed rules encode: ask an open question, then expand what he said instead of correcting it.

Saying it back, correct, is the recast. This is the single most important line on the teacher card and the one adults get wrong. He says “the dog he runned”; you say “Yes — the dog ran,” as if agreeing, and you move on. You do not say no, you do not ask him to repeat it, and you do not do it twice. A recast keeps the conversation going and still supplies the form. An announced error ends the conversation, and with a silent-period child it sometimes ends the talking.

Waiting is most of the work. An English-medium adult waits about a second. A four-year-old assembling a second-language sentence needs five or eight. If you fill the gap, he learns that if he waits long enough you will say it for him, and after a fortnight of that he will simply wait.

Set it up

  • One photo in the stand. Not a stack — a stack is a test.
  • The teacher card face up, so anybody covering your class runs the routine the same way.
  • A tick list, the same one the author’s chair keeps, so no child is quietly skipped. The children who most need this are the ones who never come and ask.

Working, or not working

It is working when
  • He adds a second sentence before you have asked anything.
  • He chooses the photo, and sometimes rejects the first one.
  • He corrects himself mid-sentence and carries on.
If you see this, do this
  • You have asked four questions. Stop. One question, “What else?”, and that is the whole permitted repertoire.
  • He says one word. Say the sentence back around his word, correct, and stop. One word is a session.
  • You caught yourself saying “no”. Say the right version as agreement instead, immediately, and do not explain what you just did.
  • He never comes. Go to him with the stand, in private, and ask. Never in front of the group.

Move on when

  • He adds a second sentence before you ask.

In one breath

The child picks a photograph, tells you about it, and hears his own sentence said back to him correctly, as though you were agreeing with him rather than fixing him.

The first fifteen minutes, step by step

This one is fifteen minutes of your day, not fifteen minutes with one child. Three or four children, three or four minutes each.

  1. Put one photo in the stand, on the tray, somewhere a child passes.
  2. When a child stops at it, sit down with him. Do not call anybody over.
  3. Say it once: “Tell me about it.”
  4. Then wait. However long that is. Count to eight in your head if it helps you not to speak.
  5. He says something. Anything. One word counts.
  6. Say his sentence back to him, correct, as if you agree: “Yes — the dog ran into the water.” Once. Do not drill it and do not ask him to say it again.
  7. Ask one thing only: “What else?”
  8. Wait again. Whatever comes, say it back correct, once.
  9. Stop when he stops. Do not fish for a third sentence.
  10. Tick his name. Change the photo when everyone has had a turn, not when you are bored of it.

Never say no. Not “no, it’s ran”, not “nearly”, not “say it again properly”. The corrected form goes in as agreement or it does not go in at all.

Nothing is written down. This is the opposite of the Writing Shelf’s author’s chair, where you scribe every word. Here there is no sheet, no book and no mark — only the photo, and him, and you not talking.

04 · The daily loop

Fifteen minutes, and four first presentations

The shelf is for individual and paired work. These are the four presentations that put it into use — one a week is plenty, and the fourth is not a presentation to a child at all, it is a habit you build in yourself.

05 · Moving on

One sign per tray. Not a calendar, a signal.

You do not move a child on because it is week six — and on this shelf least of all, because a silent-period child may spend a term on the wordless way in and then produce all of it at once.

  • 01Behind the Screen → 2. A pair completes a four-object round with no pointing, twice.
  • 02Tell It, Order It → 3. B orders all four from A’s telling alone, and A uses First and Last without the card.
  • 03Puppet Talk → 4. He says a line without looking at the card, and adds one of his own.
  • 04Tell Me About It → a conversation. He adds a second sentence before you ask.
If a child stalls

Go back to the silent way in, not forward. Every work here has one — copy an arrangement card, order a set, act the answer with the puppet — and a fortnight of doing the work without words is not lost time. The one thing that never helps is asking him to say it again.

The rule, if you only keep one line

The words must do something. A frame card stands at the back of every tray; every work has a silent way in; every word is one he already owns from the Writing Shelf; and the shelf is a ladder — one word, one frame sentence, four sentences in order, a two-line exchange. Trays 1 to 3 check themselves. Tray 4 does not, and that is what it is for.
