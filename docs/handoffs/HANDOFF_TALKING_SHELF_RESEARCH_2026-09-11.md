# The Talking Shelf — research + candidate works

**Written 2026-09-11.** A research pass on a new idea Tredoux raised: alongside the
finished Writing Shelf (8 self-directed works, `ws:1`–`ws:8`, reading/writing), build a
**Talking Shelf** — works where a child alone, two children together, or a teacher and
one child, hold real English *conversations*. The constraint that makes this hard: the
work must be **primarily self-directed**, Montessori-style — the material carries the
lesson and checks itself, the child chooses it and repeats it, an adult is not standing
over every turn. Teacher-led drill is not the answer; that already exists, and it does
not scale to a room of twenty and does not produce the same ownership the Writing Shelf
gets from its own control of error.

This document does three things: says why the gap is real, reports what the research
literature actually supports (as opposed to what's fashionable), and proposes 6–8
candidate works in the house format, ranked, so a future session can go straight to
building the highest-value one first.

---

## 1. Why / the gap

Two things were checked directly before assuming a gap exists.

**Classic Montessori does not have a formal "conversation work."** Trillium Montessori,
Montessori Services and Montessori Northshore all describe oral language in the 3–6
classroom the same way: it happens *everywhere and all the time* — morning greeting,
circle time, snack-table vocabulary, sharing day, story time — modeled by the adult and
picked up by osmosis. There is no shelf material whose job is specifically spoken
conversation, and no built-in control of error for speech the way there is for a
cylinder block or a sound-box. Grace and Courtesy lessons script *social* phrases
("may I…", "thank you") but are teacher-presented manners lessons, not open
language-production practice, and they are not self-correcting once presented. This
matches what Tredoux's own Writing Shelf already solved for *written* output — nothing
comparable exists yet for *spoken* output. That is the actual gap: not "children don't
talk enough," but "there is no self-directed material whose job is producing spoken
English and whose design lets a child (or pair) catch their own miss."

**Whale Class's children are the harder case, not the easier one.** They are Mandarin-L1,
ages 2–6, learning English as an additional language inside a Montessori room that
already gives them phonics, readers, a word-card tin, and heart words (see
`docs/curriculum/spec/MASTER_SPINE.md` and the Dark Phonics/SATPIN sequence). They can
often *read* a word before they can *say* a full sentence with it unprompted — the
classic ESL profile where decoding outruns spontaneous production. A talking shelf that
harvests vocabulary they already own (the reader word-tin, the heart-word ring, the
Dark Phonics letter sequence) rather than teaching new words is the efficient design —
it turns "words I can decode" into "words I can say to another person," which is a
different skill and, per the research below, does not develop automatically from
reading practice alone.

## 2. What the research says

**Comprehensible output — talking is a different job from listening, and young children
need the chance to do it.** Swain's output hypothesis (see the ERIC review
[A Review of the Application and Development of Output Hypothesis](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/b417/45bd42aa2743d7c6f8eaaf0529aeafc88a3c.pdf)
and the [Wikipedia overview of Comprehensible Output](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comprehensible_output))
argues that *producing* language — not just receiving it — forces a learner to notice
gaps in what they can actually say, test hypotheses, and get feedback, and that none of
that happens from listening/reading input alone. This is a mature, well-cited theory in
adult and school-age SLA; direct RCT evidence at ages 3–6 specifically is thinner (most
output-hypothesis studies are done with older learners), so the honest read is: strong
theoretical case, real for young children too by extension and classroom observation,
but not something with the same weight of preschool-specific trial evidence as, say,
dialogic reading has.

**Dialogic reading works, and has been tested with additional-language learners.** A
2024 pragmatic randomized trial (Tandfonline: [Dialogic reading in preschool, enrolling
additional language learners](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00313831.2024.2348473))
and the older What Works Clearinghouse review
([WWC Intervention Report — Dialogic Reading](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/InterventionReports/WWC_Dialogic_Reading_020807.pdf))
both support it as an oral-language booster for young children, including L2 learners.
Dialogic reading is adult-led (the "PEER" prompt-evaluate-expand-repeat sequence), so it
is not itself a self-directed shelf work — but it validates the underlying mechanism the
candidate works below borrow: an adult (or, engineered into a material, a fixed prompt
sequence) that asks an open question and expands the child's answer, rather than simply
correcting it.

**Information-gap and barrier games are the best-evidenced *self-checking* structure for
spoken output.** The Bell Foundation's own EAL guidance on
[information gap activities](https://www.bell-foundation.org.uk/resources/great-ideas/information-gap-activities/)
and [barrier games](https://www.bell-foundation.org.uk/resources/great-ideas/barrier-games/),
plus the general linguistics literature on the
[information gap task](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_gap_task), describe the
same shape every time: two people each hold information the other lacks, a physical
barrier prevents peeking, and the task can only be completed by talking. Crucially for a
Montessori shelf: **the barrier itself is a control of error**. Nothing has to be marked
right or wrong by an adult — when the screen comes down, the two trays either match or
they don't, and the children see it themselves. This is the single most Montessori-shaped
piece of research found — it produces a genuine control of error for TALKING the same
way a knobbed cylinder produces one for a hand. The Bell Foundation notes it scales
across ages and proficiency levels, including pairing a stronger speaker with an
emerging one.

**Sentence frames and formulaic chunks are how young / low-proficiency learners actually
start producing language, not a crutch to be avoided.** Wray's work on
[formulaic language in acquisition and production](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ659409.pdf)
and classroom guidance from Colorín Colorado
([sentence frames and sentence starters](https://www.colorincolorado.org/teaching-ells/ell-classroom-strategy-library/sentence-frames))
both support giving an ESL child a fixed frame ("I see a ___", "Can I have the ___,
please?") to fill rather than asking for free generation from nothing — this lowers the
cognitive load enough that a 3–6 EAL child can actually get a sentence out, and repeated
use of the frame is exactly how the frame itself stops being scaffolding and becomes
internalized grammar. This maps directly onto the sentence-building materials Tredoux
already has running (Writing Shelf Tray 5's word-card tin, and the reader passages) —
speech frames should reuse the SAME words those trays already use.

**Recasts, not overt correction, are the right adult move for this age — and a
recorded-playback comparison is a way to get a "recast" without an adult in the loop.**
The literature on [recasts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recast_(language_teaching)) —
an adult repeating the child's utterance back in corrected form, without announcing an
error — is long-standing in child SLA, on the logic that overt correction shuts down
production while a recast keeps the conversation going and still supplies the correct
form. For a *self-directed* material, the nearest equivalent is recording the child's own
sentence and then letting them hear a clean model spoken by a teacher or a native
recording, immediately after — this is documented as a real self-awareness tool for
pronunciation in older L2 learners (see the academic sample-activities piece,
["Audio Recordings as a Self-Awareness Tool"](https://www.academia.edu/8019666/Audio_Recordings_as_a_Self_Awareness_Tool_for_Improving_Second_Language_Pronunciation_in_the_Phonetics_and_Phonology_Classroom_Sample_Activities),
and general practitioner writing at
[Sanako, "The power of voice recording in language teaching"](https://sanako.com/the-power-of-voice-recording-in-language-teaching)).
Evidence at ages 3–6 specifically is again thin — most of this literature is
secondary-school and adult phonetics classrooms — so treat "record and compare" as a
promising, low-risk mechanism borrowed from older-learner research, not a
preschool-validated intervention in its own right.

**The silent period is real and matters for design.** Krashen's original claim and its
follow-up examination (Gibbons 1985,
["The Silent Period: An Examination"](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-1770.1985.tb01027.x),
plus practitioner summaries at
[Eslbase](https://www.eslbase.com/teaching/silent-period-second-language-acquisition/) and
[EAL Diaries](https://ealdiaries.com/2025/01/understanding-the-silent-period/)) — a young
child newly immersed in a second language may go weeks or months producing almost
nothing while absorbing input, and forcing early speech can backfire (anxiety, shutdown,
occasionally mistaken for selective mutism, per the ASHA Leader piece on
["When a Child Goes Silent"](https://leader.pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/leader.FTR1.19112014.34)).
Design consequence: **every talking work needs a non-verbal or minimal-verbal entry
point** — pointing, matching, one-word answers accepted as valid completion — so a
silent-period child can still do the work and build toward speech at their own pace,
rather than being excluded from the shelf until they're "ready to talk."

**AI voice tools for child speech exist, but the evidence and the safety case are not
there yet for a self-directed Montessori shelf, and this document recommends against
building one now.** Buddy.ai (profiled at [Unite.AI](https://www.unite.ai/buddy-ai-children-language-learning-speech-recognition/))
claims child-specific speech recognition that beats general-purpose ASR on children's
voices, with a curriculum-locked, no-open-chat design and real-time-discard privacy —
which is the *responsible* end of a market that also includes toddlers being handed
open-ended ChatGPT voice mode with no guard rails (Futurism,
["Parents Are Giving Their Toddlers ChatGPT on Voice Mode"](https://futurism.com/artificial-intelligence/parents-toddlers-chatgpt-voice-mode))
and general child-safety warnings from AACAP
(["Is AI Dangerous for Children?"](https://www.aacap.org/AACAP/Families_and_Youth/Facts_for_Families/FFF-Guide/AI_and_Children-145.aspx)).
Carnegie Mellon's December 2025 work letting kids hear their own voice "corrected" by AI
([CMU News](https://www.cmu.edu/news/stories/archives/2025/december/speaking-their-language-cmus-new-ai-lets-kids-hear-their-own-corrected-voice))
is a research prototype, not a deployed classroom tool. None of this is mature enough,
private enough, or cheap enough to belong on a shelf a 3-year-old chooses unsupervised —
flagged as an open decision in §6, not built into any candidate work.

## 3. Design principles for self-directed talking works

Distilled from the above, applied the same way the Writing Shelf's rules were applied:

1. **The control of error must be visible without an adult judging speech.** A barrier
   coming down, a recording played back, a sequence checked against a card back, a
   scripted line printed on the far side of a card — never "the teacher says whether it
   was right."
2. **Reuse words the child already owns.** Pull from the reader word-tin, the heart-word
   ring, and the current Dark Phonics letter — a talking work is not where new
   vocabulary is taught, it's where known vocabulary becomes spoken sentences.
3. **A frame, not a blank page.** Every work hands the child a sentence frame or a
   scripted exchange to fill, per the formulaic-language research — free generation from
   nothing is a Tray 7 (teacher-scribed) job, not a shelf job.
4. **A silent-period entry point.** Pointing, matching, or a one-word answer must count
   as a legitimate first pass through the work.
5. **Pair works need a real barrier**, not just "sit together" — a screen, a folder
   stood on its edge, backs turned. Without it, the stronger talker in the pair does all
   the work and the material stops checking anything.
6. **No adult required to START the work** — even the teacher+child work below has to be
   choosable by the child walking up with a card, the way Tray 7's chair works, not a
   summons.

## 4. Candidate works, ranked

### 1. Twin Baskets — "Same or Different?" (pair, solo-adaptable)

**On the tray:** two small matching baskets, each holding 5–6 miniatures drawn straight
from Writing Shelf Trays 1/2's object sets (cat, pig, dog, bed, mug…) — identical except
**one deliberately swapped item** in one basket. One free-standing card screen.

**The child does:** two children sit either side of the screen, each with one basket.
Taking turns, each describes their objects aloud one at a time ("I have a dog. I have a
red mug.") while the other checks whether they have the same thing. When they think
they've found the ONE that's different, they say so — then lift the screen and check.

**Control of error:** the screen coming down. The trays either match on everything but
one item, or they don't — no adult adjudicates.

**You say once:** "You can't look. You have to tell each other."

**Have · Buy · Make:** Have — the Tray 1/2/6 miniature sets already exist. Buy — a
simple wooden or card screen (already used for the sound-frame mat storage, repurpose
the idea). Make — nothing new; this is the cheapest work on the list.

**15-minute presentation:** teacher plays BOTH roles with the screen up first, thinking
aloud ("I have a pig… do you have a pig?"), then puts two children on either side and
steps back.

**Move on when:** the pair completes a round without a peek, in full sentences rather
than single words, and correctly identifies the swapped item from speech alone.

### 2. Story Retell Relay (pair — reuses Tray 6 material, zero new art)

**On the tray:** the SAME three lettered envelopes Tray 6 already uses (seed→flower,
egg→hen, apple→core, 4 frames each) — a second, spoken use of an existing material.

**The child does:** Child A studies one sequence silently, then closes the envelope and
tells Child B the story out loud, in order, from memory — no pictures visible to either
child during the telling. Child B repeats it back in their own words. Then they open the
envelope together and lay the four pictures out to check the telling matched the true
order.

**Control of error:** the four pictures themselves, checked against the story just told,
by the children, at the end.

**You say once:** "Tell it — don't show it."

**Have · Buy · Make:** entirely have — this doubles the existing Tray 6 sets as a talking
work with a laminated instruction card as the only addition.

**Move on when:** the story is told in more than one sentence, includes a beginning-
middle-end, and Child B's retell keeps the events in the same order.

---

### 3. Puppet Script Cards (solo or pair)

**On the tray:** 2 hand puppets, 6–8 laminated script cards. Front: a two-line scripted
exchange using known sentence frames and heart words ("Hello! What is your name? — My
name is ___."). Back: nothing — the printed line on the front IS the control of error;
the child reads it (or has it read to them) and performs it exactly.

**The child does:** picks a card, picks a puppet (or hands the second puppet to a
partner), reads/recites the line, the puppet "answers" with the printed reply. A child in
the silent period can perform the puppet's ACTIONS (nod, wave) while a partner or the
teacher voices the line — a legitimate first pass.

**Control of error:** the printed script. Nothing is graded; the work either matches the
card or the child notices it didn't and tries again.

**You say once:** "Say what's on the card."

**Have · Buy · Make:** Buy — two simple hand puppets. Make — 6–8 script cards, house
font/size per the print-rules doc (§8.2 mounted-card sizes), reusing Dark Phonics/reader
vocabulary already illustrated in the picture bank, so **art needed = zero new MJ
images**, only layout.

**Move on when:** the child chooses the work unprompted, performs a script from memory
without looking at the card, and — the real target — improvises ONE line beyond the
script that still makes sense.

---

### 4. Recorded Read-and-Compare (solo)

**On the tray:** a simple, large-button child's audio recorder (or a locked-down tablet
recording app — see §6), a small stand of reader passage cards, one pair of
headphones, and pre-recorded "model" tracks of a teacher reading the same 6–8 passages
cleanly.

**The child does:** picks a card, records themselves reading it aloud, then puts on
headphones and listens to the teacher's model recording of the same passage, then
listens to their own. No correction is spoken by anyone.

**Control of error:** the child's own ears, against a clean model, immediately after
their own attempt — the nearest self-directed equivalent to a recast (§2).

**You say once:** "Listen to yours. Then listen to mine."

**Have · Buy · Make:** Buy — a child-safe recorder (hardware decision, see §6). Make — 6–8
teacher-recorded model tracks, and this is the ONE candidate work that needs new AUDIO
production, not new art (reuses existing reader passages, zero new pictures).

**Move on when:** the child does this unprompted for a passage he chose, and can name ONE
thing he'd like to try differently before recording again — self-monitoring, not just
listening.

---

### 5. Ask-Me Spinner (pair)

**On the tray:** a simple card spinner with 6 question stems around the edge ("What's
your favourite ___?" "Do you like ___?" "Where is the ___?"), and a matching set of
answer-frame cards, colour-coded to the spinner wedge they answer ("I like ___." "Yes, I
like ___. / No, I don't like ___." "The ___ is ___.").

**The child does:** spins with a partner, asks the question the spinner lands on using
the printed stem, the partner answers using the colour-matched frame card, then they
swap turns.

**Control of error:** the colour-match between the spun question and the required
answer-frame — a wrong-shaped answer is visibly off-colour, not just "wrong."

**You say once:** "Match the colour."

**Have · Buy · Make:** Buy — a spinner base (cheap classroom-supply item). Make — 6
question wedges + matching answer cards, reusing reader/heart-word vocabulary.

**Move on when:** both children complete 5 rounds without needing the colour cue, and one
child asks a spinner question of a THIRD child unprompted (transfer beyond the pair).

---

### 6. Show-and-Tell Card, one-on-one with the teacher (adult + child, semi-scripted)

**On the tray:** a small stand of "prompt cards" (a photo or a simple object) plus one
sentence-frame reminder card ("This is a ___. It is ___. I like it because ___.").

**The child does:** picks a prompt, sits with the teacher (Tray 7's chair works well for
this too — same fixture, second use), describes it using the frame, and the teacher's
ONLY job is to ask ONE open follow-up question and, if the child's sentence had an error,
say it back correctly without naming the mistake — a recast, per §2, not a correction.

**Control of error:** none, by design — this is the one work that is not fully
self-directed, and it is ranked lowest of the "built" works for exactly that reason. It
exists because the research (dialogic reading, recasts) is strongest for adult-mediated
talk, and a real Montessori room still has 1:1 moments; it should be thought of as a
**bridge work**, timetabled like Tray 7, not shelved as fully independent.

**You say once (teacher's line):** "Tell me about it. What else can you tell me?"

**Have · Buy · Make:** entirely have — reuses the Tray 7 chair and needs only the prompt
cards.

**Move on when:** the child adds a second sentence unprompted, or picks a prompt and
starts talking before being asked.

---

### 7 (flagged, not recommended yet). AI Voice Practice Booth

Per §2's research, this is NOT recommended for build now: the strongest child-specific
product (Buddy.ai) is proprietary SaaS with real-time voice processing that would need
its own privacy review for a room of 2–6-year-olds, the CMU self-correction prototype is
a research demo, and the general market (unrestricted ChatGPT voice mode handed to
toddlers) is a documented cautionary tale, not a model to follow. If Tredoux wants to
revisit this later, the design principles above (control of error visible, no open-ended
chat, model recording rather than live generative dialogue) point toward something closer
to candidate #4 (recorded model comparison) with pre-scripted, curriculum-locked prompts
— not a live conversational AI.

## 6. Open decisions for Tredoux

1. **Build order.** This document ranks Twin Baskets and Story Retell Relay first because
   they need zero new materials and have the cleanest control of error. Confirm that
   order, or pick a different starting work.
2. **How many works make a "shelf."** The Writing Shelf is 8 trays. Does the Talking
   Shelf need to reach a similar number before it's real, or should 3–4 works (say,
   #1, #2, #3, #5) ship first as a proof, with #4 and #6 added once there's a device/
   recorder decision?
3. **Hardware for #4.** A dedicated child's audio recorder (simple, robust, no screen) vs
   a locked-down tablet recording app — cost, durability with 2–6-year-olds, and whether
   it needs charging/syncing infrastructure the room doesn't have yet.
4. **Is #6 (teacher+child) actually a shelf work, or a scheduled routine** like Tray 7's
   author's-chair slot? This document leans routine, not shelf, since it has no
   independent control of error — confirm or override.
5. **Pairing mechanics.** Several works need TWO children of compatible level. Does the
   room's existing partner-choosing convention (however Tray 3/other paired activities
   already handle it, if any) carry over, or does this need its own rule (e.g. a
   stronger talker paired with an emerging one, per the Bell Foundation's guidance)?
6. **Vocabulary source of truth.** Confirm that talking-work sentence frames and prompt
   words should pull from the CURRENT reader word-tin / heart-word ring / current Dark
   Phonics letter (per `docs/circle-time/YEAR_CALENDAR_2026-27.md`'s weekly letter), the
   same way Writing Shelf Tray 4/5 do — or whether the Talking Shelf should run on its
   own separate, simpler word list.
7. **AI voice tool** — confirmed hold per §2/§4's research; revisit only if Tredoux wants
   it despite the safety/evidence gap, with the scoped-down "recorded model comparison,
   no live generative chat" design as the fallback shape.
